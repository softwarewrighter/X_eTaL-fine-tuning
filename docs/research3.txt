Weight-space fine-tuning without backpropagation: emerging alternatives to LoRA
Research review • November 5, 2026

The interesting question is no longer simply whether we can fine-tune an LLM with fewer trainable parameters, as LoRA, QLoRA, VeRA, and DoRA attempt to do. It is whether we can improve a pretrained model by directly searching, editing, or composing its weights without computing gradients through the model.

That opens several distinct research directions:

Weight-space optimization: Search for useful changes to model parameters using fitness evaluations rather than backpropagation.

Evolution strategies (ES): Estimate useful update directions from populations of perturbed models and their measured performance.

Direct weight editing: Modify specific parameters, neurons, or representations to introduce or correct behavior.

Model merging and task vectors: Combine previously learned capabilities through weight arithmetic, often without additional training.

Agent-directed optimization: Let an agent propose, evaluate, and retain model modifications using external feedback such as compiler diagnostics, test results, or benchmark scores.

These approaches are not necessarily interchangeable. Some eliminate backpropagation entirely, some move it to an earlier offline stage, and others use gradients only to discover a small editable subspace.

For your X_eTaL fine-tuning work, I would treat them as a broader family of weight-space adaptation techniques, with LoRA as one baseline rather than the central architecture.

A particularly important distinction is whether a technique can improve a model using only inference access and measurable outcomes. That is where evolution strategies, black-box optimization, and agent-guided weight editing become especially interesting.

1.2 DART-ES — October 2026
Paper: Difficulty-Aware Reweighting and Targeted Replay for Fine-Tuning LLMs with Evolution Strategies

DART-ES improves evolution strategies by changing how individual training problems contribute to the optimization signal.

Instead of treating all tasks equally, it estimates their difficulty from population success rates and historical observations, then adjusts rewards and replays selected problems.

This is particularly interesting for coding models because tasks have measurable outcomes: compilation success, unit tests, complexity scores, and performance.

The authors report:

GSM8K average accuracy of 73.53%, versus 72.07% for standard ES and 73.26% for GRPO in their comparisons.

Improvements across mathematical reasoning, instruction tuning, and code generation experiments.

Lower runtime and peak GPU memory than the compared GRPO implementations.

The paper also reports experiments with a 14B model. 

arXiv

Why it matters: DART-ES offers a direct conceptual bridge between weight-space optimization and your proposed automated coding-feedback system. It can learn from externally measured success without differentiating through a compiler, test runner, or complexity grader.

1.3 Dust — October 2026
Research announcement: Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation

Dust is especially interesting because it challenges the assumption that useful credit assignment must be calculated using backpropagation.

However, Dust is not strictly a weight-space search algorithm.

Traditional ES perturbs weights. Dust perturbs intermediate activations independently across tokens, treating each token as a virtual population member.

Two ways of searching for improvements


Dust's central advantage is that it obtains many perturbation evaluations in parallel without materializing a separate weight-modified model for every population member.

The authors report that:

Large populations can approach or occasionally exceed backpropagation results in their experiments.

Larger tested models can be more population-efficient than smaller models.

Estimated gradients become better aligned with backpropagation gradients as population size grows.

Their extrapolated compute-efficiency comparison favors Dust over transformer EGGROLL by several orders of magnitude in the studied regime.

Crucially, the authors explicitly acknowledge that Dust still needs substantial compute-efficiency improvements before becoming a practical replacement for backpropagation at contemporary training scales. 

qlabs.sh

Why it matters: Dust suggests a different architecture for learning systems: use activations as the exploration space, but still produce persistent improvements to model weights.

1.4 Evolution Strategies at Scale — ICML 2026
Paper: Evolution Strategies at Scale: LLM Fine-Tuning Beyond Reinforcement Learning

This is among the strongest direct demonstrations that full-parameter LLM fine-tuning without backpropagation is feasible.

The researchers report successful evolutionary optimization of billion-parameter language models without first compressing the optimization into a reduced parameter space.

They also report advantages over their reinforcement-learning baselines in long-horizon rewards, stability, and robustness.

This matters because evolution strategies do not need a differentiable training objective. A model can be rewarded for completing a long sequence of actions, even if those actions involve tools and external systems. 

Proceedings of Machine Learning Research

For a coding agent, that could mean rewarding a model only after it has produced an implementation that compiles, passes tests, satisfies architectural constraints, and meets performance requirements.

1.5 AGZO — ICML 2026
Paper: Activation-Guided Zeroth-Order Optimization for LLM Fine-Tuning

AGZO exploits the fact that the gradient of a linear layer lies in the span of its input activations.

Rather than perturbing weights uniformly, it builds a compact subspace from forward-pass activations and restricts perturbations to that subspace.

The researchers evaluate the method on Qwen3 and Pangu models and report improvements over prior zeroth-order baselines while retaining similar peak memory consumption. 

Proceedings of Machine Learning Research

AGZO and StructZO are especially worth studying together. Both suggest that the solution to high-dimensional black-box optimization may be better geometry rather than simply larger random populations.

1.6 Neural Thickets — March 2026, with an important November challenge
Original: Neural Thickets: Diverse Task Experts Are Dense Around Pretrained Weights

The original paper proposes that sufficiently large pretrained models may already have nearby weight configurations that perform substantially better on particular tasks.

Its simple procedure samples perturbed models, selects high-performing candidates, and ensembles their predictions.

This is attractive because it avoids iterative backpropagation entirely.

But there is an important new development.

Replication challenge: Does Random Search Discover Task Experts? Revisiting Neural Thickets

Published at the end of October 2026, this follow-up finds that much of the apparent improvement may result from selection noise and overfitting to the examples used to choose the perturbations.

The authors report that, for 10 of 12 model-task combinations, a simple selection-noise model predicts more than 75% of the selected candidate's apparent reward improvement. Advantages often disappear on held-out questions, and ensembles of selected perturbations are frequently matched by repeated sampling from the unmodified base model. 

arXiv
+1

This is an important warning for your experiments: selecting the best-performing weight perturbation is not evidence of genuine learning unless the improvement survives independent evaluation.

Another category deserves separate treatment: model merging, task arithmetic, and model editing. They can modify weights without running a new backpropagation training process, but they are not necessarily backprop-free end to end. For example, merging previously fine-tuned checkpoints inherits the training costs of those checkpoints. Likewise, some model-editing techniques calculate gradients to identify what should change.

I would not classify all such methods as equivalent to ES or Dust.

4. What the newest results actually establish
There are three increasingly ambitious claims in this research.

Claim 1: Backpropagation is not required to improve pretrained LLM weights.

Strongly supported by MeZO, ES at Scale, AGZO, and StructZO. The remaining questions are efficiency, task generalization, and model scale.

Claim 2: Structured search can dramatically improve zeroth-order optimization.

Supported by AGZO and StructZO results. Their experiments suggest that informative search directions can be much more valuable than unstructured perturbations.

Claim 3: Weight-space search can generally replace backpropagation at comparable cost and quality.

Not established. Dust reports encouraging pretraining results but acknowledges substantial compute overhead. Neural Thickets has already attracted a significant negative replication result.

The field is moving quickly, but the evidence supports a narrower conclusion than some announcements imply: backprop-free adaptation is increasingly credible, while universal superiority over conventional fine-tuning remains unproven.

6. Three research questions I would prioritize
First: Can ES improve Rust coding using outcomes that backpropagation cannot directly differentiate?

This is the most immediate practical opportunity. A compiler, test suite, and complexity grader form a measurable reward system. DART-ES and ES at Scale are natural starting points.

Second: Can activation-informed zeroth-order methods make full-parameter adaptation practical on 12–24GB GPUs?

StructZO and AGZO are promising because they attempt to reduce the effective search dimensionality without relying on conventional LoRA backpropagation. Their actual runtime, memory, and quality should be benchmarked on your hardware.

Third: Can we combine weight-space evolution with activation-space learning?

A speculative hybrid could use ES to optimize external outcomes, structured ZO to refine selected parameter regions, and Dust-like activation perturbations for local learning.

That is a research hypothesis, not an established algorithm. It would require carefully separating update mechanisms and evaluating whether the added complexity provides measurable benefits.

The major change in the 2026 research landscape is that backprop-free LLM fine-tuning is no longer merely a theoretical curiosity. There are now published demonstrations of billion-parameter evolution strategies and structured zeroth-order methods that approach first-order performance on selected benchmarks.

However, I would distinguish that from the stronger claim that these methods are already preferable to LoRA in cost, quality, or scalability. That remains highly dependent on the model, hardware, and objective.

For X_eTaL, the opportunity is to make all of these methods understandable through executable mathematics, interactive perturbation visualizations, and reproducible experiments. In particular, showing how changing the search space changes learning efficiency could be more educational than simply comparing final benchmark scores.

A useful next deliverable would be a research and implementation specification for X_eTaL-fine-tuning, with one module per algorithm, the corresponding mathematical derivation, forward-only versus backprop execution traces, and a shared Rust coding-quality benchmark. That would provide both an educational system and a platform for investigating genuinely new hybrid approaches.
