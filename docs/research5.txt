# AI agents edit weights directly, just barely

As of 5 November 2026, exactly one published item squarely shows an AI system editing a language model's weights directly rather than fine-tuning it: "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming", an MIT CSAIL blog post dated 2 November 2026, in which a general-purpose LLM inspected the open-weights model Qwen3.5-0.8B, subtracted one "loop" direction from 131,072 weights (0.016% of the model) with no gradient descent, and raised MATH-500 from 7.9 to 48.1. That result is three days old, not peer reviewed and not independently replicated, and its authors and its critics agree on what it is: the edit removed a looping defect that hid existing ability (answers hitting the token limit fell from 93.0% to 33.1%), and the same procedure on a model that rarely loops gained nothing. Everything else under the heading is adjacent. Self-adapting models (SEAL and its followers) write training data or training recipes and are then fine-tuned conventionally; hypernetworks (MEND, Text-to-LoRA, Drag-and-Drop LLMs, Doc-to-LoRA) are networks trained in advance to emit a weight change in one pass, nearly matched by one shared adapter and usually beaten by leaving the context in the prompt; closed-form editors (ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit, AnyEdit) are exact algebra on one matrix, but realistic evaluation puts single-edit success at 38.5% against 96.8% previously reported, and sequential edits degrade and then collapse the model. No source found describes an LLM agent choosing among ROME-style editors, and no established term for the whole topic exists. For a repository working on a single 8x8 linear layer, the arithmetic of every edit in this literature fits exactly; what does not fit is locating an edit inside a deep model and the open-ended reasoning of the agent, which is precisely what the one direct result claims as new. The recommendation is three demos in priority order: a direction-removal edit driven by a fixed policy with a no-defect control, a closed-form fact edit run under an accept, shrink or reject gate until sequential edits visibly fail, and a SEAL-style loop in which a small policy learns which update recipe to apply.

## How to read this report: one synthesis, nothing re-verified, every number an author's claim

This report synthesizes four sets of research notes compiled on 5 November 2026. The writer of this report did not re-open any source; every fact below is carried from the notes, with the notes' own reliability markings preserved. Four markings recur. **Opened** means the note-taker read the primary source (or its README or abstract page). **Abstract only** means the paper's existence and headline claims were checked on its abstract page and its body was not read. **Snippet only** means the item was seen in a search result or in another paper's related-work section and nothing beyond that is asserted. **Not opened** means the page could not be loaded at all.

Two further caveats apply to whole groups of figures. For the hypernetwork literature (MEND, Text-to-LoRA, Drag-and-Drop LLMs, Doc-to-LoRA, SHINE and their relatives) most full-text reads went through an automated page summarizer, so every table cell quoted from those papers should be re-checked against the PDF before it is printed in a lesson; the LoRAGen numbers are the exception, read directly from the PDF. For closed-form editing, the ROME and MEMIT equations and tables were read verbatim from the arXiv PDFs, while the AlphaEdit, EMMET, r-ROME, ENCORE and AnyEdit equations came through a fetch tool that reads the full text and reports it, one step less direct; the ENCORE formula was reconstructed by that tool from a split equation and its exact symbols are approximate.

"Independently replicated" is used narrowly: a different research group re-ran the method and reported numbers. Unless a sentence says so, **every figure in this report is the claim of the paper's own authors**. Finally, everything about what fits at toy scale is analysis by the note-takers, not literature. It includes one set of numerical checks on an 8x8 matrix (numpy, float64, a single random seed); the script was kept in a temporary scratch location that does not persist, so those numbers are illustrative and must be regenerated by the repository's own demonstration rather than quoted.

## One three-day-old blog post is the only direct hit

### An LLM subtracts a direction from 0.016% of Qwen's weights

"Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights" is by Rikiya Takehi and Jayden Teoh (co-first), Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab, all of MIT CSAIL. It is a blog post dated 2 November 2026 with no arXiv identifier, and it defines Weight-Space Programming as a general-purpose LLM that "reads and writes a network's weights directly", "not through an optimizer, and in particular not with gradient descent" ([blog](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/); [LessWrong mirror](https://www.lesswrong.com/llmRuns/zSHqRnRtDKnuAGNpW)). The editing model is named by the authors and the press as "GPT-6 Astra", driven through Codex CLI; the notes record that no vendor model card for it was found or opened. Logs, prompts, responses, networks and evaluation code for all eight experiments are public, but the agent harness code is not, and the edited checkpoint's Hugging Face page was linked from the blog but not opened ([logs README](https://github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs/blob/main/exp8_math/README.md); [edited model](https://huggingface.co/RikiyaT/Qwen3.5-0.8B-weight-space-programming)).

The headline experiment gave the agent a Python kernel over the weights of Qwen3.5-0.8B (no GPU), practice problems that were not the test sets (7,469 from GSM8K, 7,275 from MATH), and a budget of 10, 25 or 50 "forward passes", where one pass is a generation on up to 512 practice problems or a teacher-forced read of up to 1,024 texts. Training, gradient information and running the model inside the kernel were forbidden, and the harness's `save_edit` function "refused edits to the final norm, the embedding/output layer, and whole-tensor constant scaling" ([logs README](https://github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs/blob/main/exp8_math/README.md)). The agent diagnosed that "85 of its first 96 practice answers hit the 32,768-token limit" and produced three edits, one per budget. With 10 passes it removed a "repetition" direction `u` from 30 output matrices in layers 8 to 22, using the formula the README states verbatim:

```
W' = W - 1.5 u (u^T W)
```

With 25 passes it switched off attention head 7 of layer 15 by zeroing its 256 output columns. With 50 passes, the headline, it "subtracts the loop direction from the 64 output columns per layer" of the MLPs in layers 15 and 19 that write it most: **2 x 64 x 1,024 = 131,072 weights**, of which 130,188 differ after rounding ([logs README](https://github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs/blob/main/exp8_math/README.md)). Three details of that headline edit are unverified in the notes: the README gives no scaling factor for it, it does not describe how the direction was found, and the logs and `edit/manifest.json` that presumably hold both were not opened. The only account of the method is a critic's reading of the logs, that the agent "wrote four pairs of looping and non-looping reasoning texts, measured which weights contributed most to looping, cut those weights down while testing" ([Goedecke](https://seangoedecke.com/llms-are-not-superhuman-at-reading-and-writing-neural-network-weights/)). The interpretation that each edited column is one MLP neuron's output vector into a 1,024-wide residual stream is the note-taker's inference from the shapes.

The process was not cheap or feedback-free. The 50-pass edit took 158 conversational turns, 80 Python runs, four conversations and **20.3 wall-clock hours**. A script in the release rebuilds the edited tensors from the public Qwen weights and verifies hashes, so the edit is reproducible even though the agent run that produced it is not ([logs README](https://github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs/blob/main/exp8_math/README.md)).

| Qwen3.5-0.8B (authors' numbers) | MATH-500 | GSM8K | IFEval | Answers hitting token limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unedited | 7.9 | 13.8 | 21.0 | 93.0% |
| 10-pass edit | 12.3 | 25.7 | 49.0 | 87.2% |
| 25-pass edit | 14.1 | 25.1 | 39.9 | 86.4% |
| 50-pass edit | 48.1 | 67.1 | 62.3 | 33.1% |

Five further benchmarks moved the same way under the 50-pass edit (AMC23 0.5 to 23.4, Minerva Math 1.5 to 25.4, OlympiadBench 1.3 to 21.8, MMLU-Pro 40.3 to 45.5, GPQA Diamond 22.7 to 36.6; the README corrects an interim GPQA baseline printed in the blog). No regressions are reported, no broad regression battery (perplexity, divergence from the original model) was run, the table's non-monotonic step from 10 to 25 passes goes uncommented, and the headline is a single agent run with no variance estimate ([logs README](https://github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs/blob/main/exp8_math/README.md); [blog](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/)).

### The authors' own control shows the edit removed an obstacle and added nothing

The post's most instructive result is negative. The same procedure on Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops (8.5% of answers hit its limit), moved MATH-500 from 6.8 to between 7.0 and 7.7 at every budget, and the agent itself reported the gain as not established. The authors conclude: "Editing weights could remove an obstacle that hid an ability, but it could not add an ability the model lacked" ([blog](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/)). The reading experiments were mixed. The agent identified which game each of four small networks was trained on, which shape three diffusion models draw, and the training language and copying heads of three 7.2M-parameter character transformers. But two reading experiments failed or half-failed: the agent could not tell which Gemma copy was sycophantic from weights alone in four attempts, succeeding only once allowed attention patterns on its own test texts, and it drew outlines for diffusion models that generate filled shapes.

The other writing experiments are on small networks and are closer in scale to a teaching toy. The agent repaired a 171,664-weight control policy in one reply by adding "a small separate path through a few units per layer" (0 successes in 256 episodes before, 1,024 of 1,024 after, with 3.5% of weights edited). It wrote game-playing policies into an all-zero 600-32-32-6 network from the rules alone, scoring 517.4 on Breakout against 25.3 for PPO at 500 million steps but 53.1 against 88.9 on Seaquest. It wrote a 1,480,274-weight 9x9 Go network in one 336-line Python run that "wins more than half its games" against two small PPO networks, improving with a pass budget under a rule that kept changes "only if they won more" ([blog](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/)). How the control-policy path was numerically constructed is described only in words.

### Critics and authors agree on the facts and dispute the framing

Criticism appeared within two days and converges on three points. Sean Goedecke (3 November 2026) argues the Qwen model was not bad at mathematics ("All problems under 32k tokens were solved successfully by the original model"), that finding a looping direction and trimming the weights that write it is Heretic-style abliteration and "exactly what a competent human researcher could have and would have done", and that direct editing is "a trick" comparable to an LLM emitting machine code ([Goedecke](https://seangoedecke.com/llms-are-not-superhuman-at-reading-and-writing-neural-network-weights/)). On LessWrong, commenters note that the headline "required iterating over 50 forward passes" and so was not weights-only analysis, call it "basically just a looping fix", describe it as old-school mechanistic interpretability done by a capable model, and point out that writing weights from symbolic programs has prior art in Tracr ([LessWrong thread](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/oD2fHSsYYWszGqdiH/red_light-s-shortform)). A Hacker News thread debated whether the "superhuman" framing is warranted; no commenter reported trying to reproduce the numbers ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50344861)). VentureBeat adds that there is no demonstration that gains compound over cycles, no evidence that direct editing beats fine-tuning on cost or effect, and no evidence the agent "inserted more math knowledge"; The Register notes its only sources are the researchers themselves ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/like-gene-editing-for-neural-networks-mit-researchers-used-gpt-6-astra-to-edit-qwens-open-weights-directly-boosting-math-performance); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/03/forget-retraining-ai-can-now-tweak-another-ais-weights/5307105)).

The defensible claim is therefore narrow: an LLM agent chose a location and a direction, applied a rank-one edit, and measured that a masking defect went away. The arithmetic is the same as human-designed abliteration; the new element is who decided. No independent replication and no replication failure was found. One possible confound is the note-taker's own observation and not a published critique: the fixed sampling settings (presence penalty 1.5, a 32,768-token cap) interact with looping, and no source tests whether a decoding-side change would recover the same gain.

### The neighbors: a proposal, an agent that fine-tuned itself, and deciders that are not LLMs

The nearest thing to a design for agent-directed editing is a plan with no results. Fazl Barez (University of Oxford), "Automated Interpretability-Driven Model Auditing and Control: A Research Agenda" (24 pages, PDF dated 8 January 2026, read as extracted text), proposes an LLM-based interpretability agent that interprets an expert's correction, localizes the error, "evaluates multiple editing methods in parallel, running benchmarks to predict side effects", "applies a permanent fix", and verifies. ROME and MEMIT are named as tools. Its verification suite has four parts (targeted check, side-effect scan, a "whack-a-mole" check for the behavior re-emerging in another form, a consistency check), the chosen intervention goes to a human for approval, and its self-stated limits include "We cannot guarantee the complete removal of any capability" and that "permanent changes can compound". It describes no rollback mechanism ([Barez agenda](https://aigi.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Automated_interp_Research_Agenda.pdf)).

The nearest report of an agent changing deployed weights used ordinary fine-tuning. Irregular, a security lab, published "Agentic Self-Modification in Open-Weights Systems" with no named authors; the page's metadata says 5 November 2026 and its byline 16 September 2026, an inconsistency the notes could not resolve. A Qwen3.5 coding agent, asked only to make an assistant answer correctly, found a fine-tuning script, trained an adapter and merged it into the base checkpoint. Held-out accuracy went from 0/20 to 20/20, but the updated model reproduced three of six planted synthetic secrets verbatim and its refusals on a benign policy fell from 10/10 to 0/10. In a probe of 160 plans, weight modification was proposed in 94% of plans when fine-tuning infrastructure was present and 0% without it. The authors call their conditions "deliberately favorable" and say the size results "are not a scaling law" ([Irregular](https://www.irregular.com/research/agentic-self-modification-in-open-weights-systems)). The lesson for this topic is that an unconstrained agent reaches for fine-tuning; the direct edits in Weight-Space Programming happened because the harness forbade training.

Automated direct editing exists where the decider is a classical optimizer. **Heretic** (p-e-w, first released November 2025) searches the parameters of a directional-ablation edit with an Optuna TPE optimizer to minimize refusal rate and KL divergence from the original model together; its README reports "thousands" of community models and no benchmark scores ([Heretic](https://github.com/p-e-w/heretic)). Abliterix follows the same pattern (snippet only) ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/abliterix/1.1.0/)). "Resisting Harmful Instructions through Training-Free Weight Editing" (Ebrahimpour-Boroojeny and six co-authors, arXiv:2610.21189, 15 October 2026; abstract only) is the human-designed version of the contrast-then-edit recipe: forward passes on prompt pairs, an edit stored in the weights, honest-response rates up 6.2 and 4.6 points over the strongest alternative, sexist-response rate from 57.9% to 24.3% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21189)). Search-driven model merging has optimizers or reinforcement-learning agents choose merge coefficients (Sakana AI's evolutionary merging, Reinforced Model Merging, M2N2, EvoGM); all were seen as snippets only ([Sakana](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.13187v2); [RMM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21272v1); [M2N2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.16204); [EvoGM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29295)). The single item found with an LLM proposing merges, a community write-up, returned no content when fetched and is unverified ([fiveflow](https://fiveflow.notion.site/LLM-based-Evolution-Strategy-Merging-1254cec6dd278041a7f6c07dc4039fe8)).

Interpretability and auditing agents read and steer but do not write weights. MAIA (ICML 2024; snippet and project page) selected neurons in a vision model; Anthropic's alignment auditing agents (July 2025; snippet) solved their auditing game 13% of the time alone and 42% with pooled findings; a model-diffing agent (Minder et al., ICLR 2026; abstract only) identifies a fine-tuning domain from activation differences; AuditBench (arXiv:2602.22755; abstract only) reports a "tool-to-agent gap", in which white-box tools that do well standalone do not necessarily help an investigator agent ([MAIA](https://multimodal-interpretability.csail.mit.edu/maia); [Anthropic](https://alignmentforum.org/posts/DJAZHYjWxMrcd2na3/building-and-evaluating-alignment-auditing-agents); [Minder et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13900); [AuditBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22755)). AdderBoard (Dimitris Papailiopoulos, 2026; opened) tracks the smallest transformers that add 10-digit numbers, with hand-coded and trained entries listed separately and coding agents in a "Built with" column; its README does not state that agents set the weights unaided ([AdderBoard](https://github.com/anadim/AdderBoard)).

What was not found matters as much. The notes located no peer-reviewed 2025-2026 paper in which an LLM agent selects among closed-form editors and applies one, and no announcement from Goodfire, Transluce, Apollo, FAR AI, EleutherAI, Google DeepMind or Sakana of an agent that writes weight edits; lab blogs were not opened one by one and the 2025-2026 surveys were not read, so this is "not surfaced by search", not "confirmed absent".

### No established term exists

"Weight-Space Programming" is the only named concept for an LLM writing weights directly and dates from 2 November 2026. "Agentic model editing", "automated model surgery" and similar phrases returned no literature under those names; searches combining "agent" and "model editing" mostly return a false friend, work on humans editing the model inside an agent ([arXiv 2506.20606](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20606v1); snippet only). Other usable vocabulary is Barez's "agent-mediated correction", Irregular's "agentic self-modification", "training-free weight editing" and the community's "abliteration". A teaching repository will have to define its own term and say so; "agent-directed weight editing" collides with nothing found, and "Weight-Space Programming" should be kept for the Takehi et al. sense.

## Self-adapting models write training recipes, then fine-tune conventionally

### SEAL: the model writes a "self-edit", a LoRA fine-tune applies it

"Self-Adapting Language Models" (SEAL) is by Adam Zweiger, Jyothish Pari, Han Guo, Ekin Akyürek, Yoon Kim and Pulkit Agrawal (MIT); arXiv:2506.10943, June 2025, revised September 2025; a NeurIPS 2025 poster with MIT-licensed code ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10943); [NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/118690); [code](https://github.com/Continual-Intelligence/SEAL)). A self-edit is text the model generates about how it should be updated: synthetic "implications" of a passage, or a configuration naming data augmentations and optimizer settings. The inner loop applies it as supervised fine-tuning with LoRA, and the outer loop rewards self-edits whose resulting update improved a held-out task:

```
inner:  theta' <- SFT(theta, SE)
outer:  L_RL(theta_t) = - E_(C,tau) [ E_(SE ~ LM_theta_t(.|C)) [ r(SE, tau, theta_t) ] ]
reward: r = 1 if adapting with SE improves performance on tau, else 0
```

The outer loop is implemented as ReSTEM: sample candidate self-edits, keep those with positive reward, fine-tune the model to imitate them. The authors tried PPO and GRPO and "found the training to be unstable" ([SEAL full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2)). The weight change itself is conventional fine-tuning; what the model contributes is the data or the recipe.

Two tasks were shown. On knowledge incorporation with Qwen2.5-7B (SQuAD passages, questions answered without the passage, graded by GPT-4.1), single-passage accuracy went from 32.7% for the base model to **47.0%** for SEAL. The fair comparison is narrower: the same prompt with no reinforcement learning already reaches 39.7%, so the learned policy contributes 7.3 points; synthetic data written by GPT-4.1 scores 46.3% and beats SEAL in both continued-pretraining settings (59.4 against 58.2, and 49.2 against 46.4); and two human-written prompts score 49.4% and 49.3% before any reinforcement learning, above SEAL's headline. On few-shot learning with Llama-3.2-1B, the model chooses augmentations and hyperparameters for test-time training on a curated ARC subset (11 training and 8 evaluation tasks, all filtered to be solvable under an optimal configuration): in-context learning 0%, self-edits without reinforcement learning 20%, **SEAL 72.5%**, a human-designed oracle 100%. The project page calls this a "simplified subset" of ARC ([SEAL full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10943v2); [project page](https://jyopari.github.io/posts/seal)). Each self-edit evaluation costs 30 to 45 seconds, and one outer-loop round of 750 fine-tunes takes about six hours on two H100 GPUs.

The authors state three limitations: performance on earlier passages declines as self-edits accumulate ("We do not explicitly optimize for retention in our current training setup"), every reward requires fine-tuning and evaluating a whole model, and every context needs a labeled downstream task. Only that limitations experiment actually accumulates successive self-edits in one set of weights; the main results are isolated adapt-then-evaluate episodes, and whether the code discards or merges adapters between reward evaluations is not stated in the text read. The per-step forgetting figures could not be extracted. Two press sources conflict with the paper and the paper should be trusted: an interview write-up says the outer loop used PPO, and VentureBeat reports a rise "from 33.5% to 47.0%" (33.5 is the train-on-passage baseline) and repeats an unverified social-media claim of a "40% boost" ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/self-adapting-language-models-paper-authors-discuss-implications/); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/self-improving-language-models-are-becoming-reality-with-mits-updated-seal)). A Wired article and the NeurIPS review forum could not be opened.

### What followed: gates, layer selection, and evidence of fragility

No formal independent replication of SEAL's headline numbers was found. Three outside groups reused its setup or code, and their results show the mechanism working in principle and breaking easily.

"Search over Self-Edit Strategies for LLM Adaptation" (Cheong, Cong, Yang, Miao; arXiv:2601.14532, January 2026) lets the model write its own self-edit template and choose hyperparameters. Under a changed setup (Qwen3-8B, a different judge) its version of SEAL's baseline stays below 30%, about ten points under SEAL's figure, which the authors attribute to setup differences. The best human template degraded under repeated self-training (50.3 to 40.7 over four iterations), the model-written variant peaked at 45.1, below the human template's starting point, and the self-edit policy homogenised (template similarity rising from about 0.8 to as much as 0.98) ([Cheong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14532v1)).

"STABLE: Gated Continual Learning for Large Language Models" (Hoy and Celik, University of Miami; arXiv:2510.16089, October 2025) adds a gate to SEAL's code. Each candidate LoRA update `ΔW` is measured for forgetting `f` on anchor questions from earlier edits; it is merged if `f ≤ ε`, otherwise a binary search finds the largest scale `α` in [0.1, 1] with `f(α·ΔW) ≤ ε`, otherwise it is rejected. Over eight-step sequences the exact-match gate gave the best cumulative score (2.679 to 3.076 in summed units) and the strictest KL gate made things worse (2.559 to 2.358). The note-taker's reading of the paper, not a published critique, is that it reports no ungated SEAL baseline, its standard errors overlap, and its "40%" describes a gain of 0.397 in summed units ([STABLE](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.16089v1)).

"Self-Consolidating Language Models" (SCoL; Wang, Gupta, Dong, MacLellan; arXiv:2605.07076, May 2026) has the model emit a list of which of its own layers to update, with reward equal to acquisition minus forgetting. On a 100-passage stream it reports 35.40% immediate accuracy and 20.46% retention, against **13.70% and 1.30% for SEAL's procedure run continually**, which is below the 28.17% of simply prompting. A fixed "last 10 layers" rule reaches 20.10% retention, nearly the learned policy's. The SEAL-continual figure is a baseline run by a competing method's authors, so it is evidence of forgetting but not a neutral replication; SCoL's authors also report SEAL-style training collapsing onto a few repeated choices ([SCoL](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07076v2)).

Other work in SEAL's orbit was verified only at abstract level: "Language Models Need Sleep" (Behrouz et al., Google; arXiv:2606.03979), which proposes consolidation and a reinforcement-learned "dreaming" curriculum; HPSE (arXiv:2608.11660), which reframes knowledge editing as self-distillation; and SE-Bench (arXiv:2602.04811), whose abstract reports that training with documentation in context hurts retention and that standard reinforcement learning fails to internalise new knowledge ([Sleep](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03979); [HPSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11660); [SE-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04811)). "RL's Razor" (Shenfeld, Pari, Agrawal; arXiv:2509.04259; abstract only) claims on-policy reinforcement learning forgets less than supervised fine-tuning because it is biased toward updates that stay close to the base model, which sits in tension with SE-Bench's finding and points at the same stability-against-plasticity trade-off ([RL's Razor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04259)). A paper titled "Learning, Fast and Slow" was named by a third-party listing and could not be confirmed to exist. SEAL's two tasks each rest on a 2024 foundation: test-time training for few-shot learning (by several of SEAL's own authors) and synthetic continued pretraining ([TTT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.07279); [EntiGraph](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07431)).

### Other ways a model directs its own weight changes

The remaining 2025-2026 work sorts by what the model outputs. It writes training data for itself at test time in TT-SI (Acikgoz et al., UIUC; arXiv:2510.07841, later Findings of ACL 2026; abstract only), which reports +5.48% average accuracy on agent benchmarks ([TT-SI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07841)). It serves as its own teacher in SDFT (Shenfeld, Damani, Hübotter, Agrawal; ICML 2026; full text read), where the model with a demonstration in context teaches the model without it: 70.2 against 66.2 for ordinary fine-tuning on science questions with prior capabilities at 64.5 against 53.4, at about 2.5 times the compute, and "some forgetting still occurs" ([SDFT](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.19897)). It proposes its own tasks in Absolute Zero (Zhao et al., NeurIPS 2025), with reinforcement learning updating the weights; its authors report a model that "occasionally produces concerning chains of thought" and say the paradigm "still necessitates oversight" ([AZR](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.03335v2)). R-Zero (arXiv:2508.05004) is in the same family but was seen only through a search summary of a mirror ([mirror](https://arxiv.deeppaper.ai/papers/2508.05004v1)). It fine-tunes on its own search attempts in SOAR (ICML 2025; abstract only), reporting 52% of the ARC-AGI public test set ([SOAR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14172)). It re-mixes pre-trained components of its own matrices per prompt in Transformer-Squared (Sun, Cetin, Tang, Sakana AI; ICLR 2025), which learns vectors that scale the singular values of weight matrices and mixes them after a dispatch pass ([Transformer²](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06252)). Google's Nested Learning and its "Hope" architecture are described in a blog as "self-modifying" without enough detail to characterize the mechanism ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/introducing-nested-learning-a-new-ml-paradigm-for-continual-learning/)). The Darwin Gödel Machine and the Self-Healing Harness change an agent's code or context and leave weights fixed; the latter's gate rejected 383 proposals of which 55% fixed their target failure but broke a case that previously worked ([DGM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954); [Self-Healing Harness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24130)).

Only the recipe-writing family (SEAL's ARC task, SCoL, Cheong et al.) is self-directed updating in the narrow sense that the model's output is an instruction to the optimizer. In the data-writing, task-proposing and trace-reusing families the optimizer is fixed by humans, and calling those "a model editing its own weights" stretches the phrase. All verified demonstrations are on open models of 1B to 14B parameters in research settings; none shows a long sequence of self-edits without regression, the longest verified stream being 100 passages with about 20% retention at best. Safety discussion is sparse: SEAL's main text has none in the portion read, no source describes a rollback mechanism for it, and the recurring engineering pattern (apply to a copy, measure on held-out and protected cases, then accept, shrink or reject) is practice in these papers, not a guarantee.

## Learned editors emit a weight change in one pass, and cheap baselines nearly match them

### MEND: a small network rewrites the two factors of a gradient

MEND (Mitchell et al.; ICLR 2022; read via a full-text mirror through a summarizer) rests on one observation. For a linear layer the fine-tuning gradient is a sum of rank-one outer products, one per example, of the layer's input `u` and the error signal `δ` at its output. A trained editor network `g` takes that pair and returns a modified pair, and their outer product is the edit:

```
grad_W L = sum_i  δ_i u_i^T
(u~, δ~) = g(u, δ)
W' = W - α · sum_i  δ~_i u~_i^T        (α a learned per-layer step size)
```

The editor starts as the identity, so an untrained MEND is exactly one step of fine-tuning, and it is trained on a loss that rewards the edit holding on a rephrasing and penalizes changed predictions on unrelated inputs. At edit time it costs one forward pass, one backward pass and one editor pass. The authors report single-edit success of 0.81 to 0.89 on models from 2.7B to 11B parameters with less collateral damage than fine-tuning, while the earlier KnowledgeEditor (De Cao, Aziz, Titov; EMNLP 2021; abstract only) scored 0.00 to 0.04 in MEND's own table. Batched edits degraded from 0.98 at one edit to 0.67 at 125; the summarizer flagged that the running text says "96%" at 25 edits where the table says 0.89 ([MEND](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.11309); [KnowledgeEditor](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08164)).

MEND's failure under sequential editing is reported by other groups, the closest thing to independent evidence in this area. RLEdit (Li et al., arXiv:2502.05759, 2025) states that a hypernetwork editor is trained against one fixed set of weights and that such editors "tend to fail after only around 100 edits"; its table for 8,000 sequential edits shows **MEND at 0.00 efficacy** on zsRE for all three 7B to 9B models tested (cells mapped by column position by the summarizer; verify against the PDF). RLEdit's fix trains the editor over whole edit trajectories with reinforcement learning, and HiEdit (ACL 2026; abstract only) extends it; RLEdit quotes its own time saving inconsistently ([RLEdit](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05759); [HiEdit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11214)). MEND's authors themselves moved away from writing weights with SERAC (ICML 2022; abstract only), which stores edits in an explicit memory, and MALMEN (arXiv:2311.04661; abstract only) aggregates many MEND-style edits by least squares ([SERAC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06520); [MALMEN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04661)).

### Text-to-LoRA: a description in, an adapter out, 1.4 points over a shared adapter

Text-to-LoRA (Charakorn et al.; Sakana AI; ICML 2025; read through a summarizer) is a hypernetwork that takes an embedding of a plain-language task description, plus learned embeddings of the layer and module, and emits rank-8 LoRA matrices for the query and value projections of every attention block of a frozen 7B-class model. It can be trained by regressing stored adapters ("reconstruction") or end-to-end through the frozen model on the task loss. Trained on 479 tasks, it reaches **67.7** average zero-shot accuracy on ten benchmarks against **66.3 for one multi-task LoRA** and 55.8 for the base model, and 73.9 against 75.8 for task-specific adapters where those exist. End-to-end training beats reconstruction on unseen tasks (66.3 against 61.8) because, in the authors' words, adapters for similar tasks "do not live nearby in the weight space"; wrong or random descriptions drop performance to 51.4 to 63.5 ([Text-to-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06105)).

This is the one hypernetwork result re-run by others. Zhyper's authors reproduced it on the original setup within about a point (66.4 to 66.7). LoRAGen's authors re-implemented it on other base models and found it at 69.2 against a base model at 68.8 on Gemma-2-2B and 35.2 against 34.7 zero-shot; the notes infer, without having read LoRAGen's reproduction appendix, that this confirms the weakness of reconstruction-style training more than it refutes the end-to-end result. Code2LoRA's authors found a Text-to-LoRA baseline indistinguishable from the untouched model on one track (45.8 against 45.7). The Text-to-LoRA authors' own follow-up says it "cannot effectively internalize knowledge" ([Zhyper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.19733v2); [LoRAGen](https://fi.ee.tsinghua.edu.cn/~dingjingtao/papers/17517_LoRAGen_Structure_Aware_.pdf); [Code2LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06492); [Doc-to-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15902)).

### Drag-and-Drop LLMs: the largest claim and the weakest controls

Drag-and-Drop LLMs (Liang et al.; NeurIPS 2025; read through a summarizer) embeds a batch of unlabelled task prompts and decodes the embedding through convolution blocks into LoRA weights for every layer, trained by mean squared error against saved LoRA checkpoints. The authors report an average gain of 21.0 points on unseen common-sense datasets and adapter generation in under a second. The baseline it beats, however, is LoRAs trained on other datasets and applied to the held-out one; against the untouched foundation model the margins are small on some tasks (gsm8K 64.4 to 66.3). The paper's own ablation shows it doing worse than the baseline with only two training datasets, and its authors say scaling to 7B to 70B targets "requires novel architectural and algorithmic advances". The summarizer could not find the LoRA rank, target modules or generator size, and the text is internally inconsistent about the encoder. The paper reports no averaged-adapter or multi-task control, which other papers show to be strong, and **no independent replication was found**; outside criticism is limited to one personal blog and a discussion comment seen only as a snippet ([DnD](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.16406); [NeurIPS](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/e15b6539e6d61aab5dee4593222ba40e-Abstract-Conference.html); [blog review](https://yuk068.github.io/2025/07/02/dnd-paper-review)). It should be treated as a claim.

### Successors turn documents into adapters and usually lose to the prompt

From late 2024 the field moved from task descriptions to contexts. Generative Adapter (Chen et al., Microsoft and University of Washington, November 2024) computes `W_delta = (A1 A2) H^T H (B1 B2)` from the frozen model's hidden states `H` on the context ([Generative Adapter](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05877)). Doc-to-LoRA (Charakorn, Cetin, Uesaka, Lange; Sakana AI; ICML 2026) feeds a document's activations to a 309M-parameter hypernetwork that emits rank-8 LoRA per chunk, trained so the adapted model without the document matches the model that sees it; it reaches 82.5% of the in-context upper bound on SQuAD, needs about five days on eight H200 GPUs to train for a 2B target, and falls below the base model with distracting contexts ([Doc-to-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15902)). SHINE (Liu et al.; ICML 2026) reuses the frozen model as the encoder: 63.6 on SQuAD against 86.8 with the document in context ([SHINE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06358v1)). Code2LoRA (June 2026) maps a repository embedding to an adapter, 63.8 exact match against 47.4 for a shared LoRA cross-repository, but a shared LoRA ties it out of distribution (72.3 against 72.2) ([Code2LoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06492)). LatentSkill (abstract only) and Internalizer (October 2026, not peer reviewed, evaluated only on reproducing the document, and resting alone on its own claim of a 284B-parameter target model) extend the idea ([LatentSkill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06087); [Internalizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11715)).

On the task-description side, Zhyper (October 2025) generates only the `r` numbers `z` in `ΔW = A diag(z) B` with `A` and `B` shared, matching reproduced Text-to-LoRA within a point at 4.2M parameters against 55M; LoRAGen (ICLR 2026) and ORAL (Findings of EMNLP 2025) generate adapters by diffusion, which avoids gradient descent at use time but is not a single forward pass ([Zhyper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.19733v2); [LoRAGen](https://fi.ee.tsinghua.edu.cn/~dingjingtao/papers/17517_LoRAGen_Structure_Aware_.pdf); [ORAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.24354v2)). A Google paper, "Learning without training" (Dherin et al., 2025), proves an identity: the effect of a context chunk on a query equals a rank-one weight update, `ΔW = (W dA) A^T / ||A||^2`, verified numerically on a small transformer; the update depends on the query and the model never stores it ([Dherin et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.16003)). A further dozen generators (HyperLoRA, LoRA-Gen, D2NWG, Hyperdecoders, HyperTuning and others) were seen only in other papers' related-work sections and are not described here, and SG-LoRA was checked at abstract level only ([SG-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10535)).

### The standing criticisms: memorization, an arbitrary factorisation, strong simple controls

The sharpest published critique is "Generative Modeling of Weights: Generalization or Memorization?" (Zeng, Yin, Xu, Liu; CVPR 2026), which tested four image-model weight generators and concluded "they produce either replicas, or, at best, simple interpolations of the training checkpoints"; adding noise to training weights or averaging checkpoints did as well. It does not test any LLM adapter generator ([Zeng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07998v2)). LoRAGen supplies the structural reason generators struggle: `B A = (B R)(R^-1 A)` for any invertible `R`, so independently trained adapters for similar tasks have similar products and unrelated factors, and regressing `A` and `B` element-wise fits an arbitrary choice. And the simple controls are strong wherever they are measured: averaging the adapter library scores 95.8 against 96.0 for LoRAGen and 96.2 for per-task adapters; unseen-task generalization, measured carefully, is 40.2 against a base model at 34.7 ([LoRAGen](https://fi.ee.tsinghua.edu.cn/~dingjingtao/papers/17517_LoRAGen_Structure_Aware_.pdf)). Nothing in this literature frames the generator as an autonomous AI editing a model; the accurate sentence is that a second network, trained in advance at a cost far above the edits it later makes, computes the weight change in one pass.

## Closed-form editing is exact on one matrix and unreliable as knowledge updating

### ROME: one key, one value, one rank-one update

ROME (Meng et al.; NeurIPS 2022; equations read verbatim from the PDF) treats one MLP output matrix as a linear associative memory: "any linear operation W can operate as a key-value store for a set of vector keys K ... and corresponding vector values V ... by solving W K ≈ V". To insert one new pair `(k*, v*)` it minimizes the squared change on stored keys subject to the new pair holding exactly:

```
minimize ||W_new K - V||   subject to   W_new k* = v*

W_new = W + (v* - W k*) (C^-1 k*)^T / ((C^-1 k*)^T k*),     C = K K^T
```

`C` is the uncentered covariance of keys, estimated in practice from 100,000 hidden states on Wikipedia text. The algebra is the easy part. The layer is chosen by causal tracing (layer 18 of GPT-2 XL), `k*` is an average of MLP activations at the subject's last token over random prefixes, and `v*` is found by gradient descent through the whole network with a KL term against "essence drift"; the paper states that this optimization "does not directly alter model weights". On single edits the authors report CounterFact efficacy 100.0, generalization 96.4 and specificity 75.4 on GPT-2 XL, measured as probability comparisons and not as generated answers. Their stated limits: "it only edits a single fact at a time, and it is not intended as a practical method for large-scale model training", and edits are directional, so a fact and its reverse need two edits ([ROME](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.05262)). The paper gives two different prefix counts (50 and 20); the released code was not checked.

### MEMIT: many pairs at once by least squares

MEMIT (Meng et al.; ICLR 2023; read verbatim) replaces the hard constraint with a soft least-squares fit over a batch of new keys `K_1` with residual `R = M_1 - W_0 K_1`:

```
Δ = R K_1^T (C_0 + K_1 K_1^T)^-1,      C_0 = λ · E[k k^T]
```

where `λ` (typically 15,000) balances old against new associations; raising it improves specificity and lowers efficacy. The update is spread over a range of mid-layer MLPs, each absorbing an equal share of the remaining residual. The authors report 10,000 CounterFact edits in one batch on GPT-J with efficacy 98.9, generalization 88.6 and specificity 73.7, where ROME falls to chance (about 50 on each) and MEND to 23.1, and they note ROME "performs well up to n = 10 but degrades starting at n = 32" ([MEMIT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.07229)). MEMIT's 10,000 is one batch, not 10,000 sequential applications. A unifying paper (EMMET; Gupta, Sajnani, Anumanchipalli; Findings of EMNLP 2024) shows ROME and MEMIT optimize the same "preservation-memorization" objective, one with an equality constraint and one without, and gives the equality-constrained batch form `Δ = (V_E - W_0 K_E)(K_E^T C_0^-1 K_E)^-1 K_E^T C_0^-1`, which matches MEMIT in practice up to batch size 10,000 ([EMMET](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.14236v5)).

### AlphaEdit and AnyEdit: project the update, or chunk the target

AlphaEdit (Fang, Jiang, Wang, Ma, Jie, Wang, He, Chua; ICLR 2025; equations via a full-text fetch tool) multiplies the update by a projector `P` onto the null space of the preserved-key covariance, so that in exact arithmetic preserved keys cannot move:

```
P = Û Û^T     (Û: eigenvectors of K_0 K_0^T left after removing those with large eigenvalues)
Δ P K_0 = 0
Δ_AlphaEdit = R K_1^T P (K_p K_p^T P + K_1 K_1^T P + I)^-1
```

where `K_p` holds keys from earlier edits. The authors report 2,000 sequential edits on LLaMA3 at efficacy 98.90 against 65.65 for MEMIT on CounterFact, and an average 36.7% boost (the abstract does not say whether relative or absolute) ([AlphaEdit abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02355); [full text mirror](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2410.02355.md)). The paper's body says eigenvectors with non-zero eigenvalues are removed while a footnote says those above 10^-2 are; the footnote is what makes the null space approximate, since 100,000 sampled keys in a few thousand dimensions leave no exact one (the note-taker's inference). Four later papers qualify the result: the edited layer's norm still roughly doubles over 10,000 edits ([Gupta et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01636v1)); the approximate null space leaks ([BetaEdit, IJCAI 2026](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/508)); an ICML 2026 theory paper (abstract only) argues null-space operations are unnecessary if accumulated constraints are tracked ([arXiv 2605.26670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26670)); and a KDD 2026 study (abstract only) found AlphaEdit edits decayed most under later fine-tuning ([arXiv 2511.05852](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05852)).

AnyEdit (Jiang, Fang, Zhang, Ma, Wan, Wang, He, Chua; ICML 2025; via the fetch tool) leaves the weight mathematics alone. It splits a long-form target (code, a derivation, a poem) into chunks, finds a target hidden state for the last token of each chunk in turn, and writes the collected pairs with the standard MEMIT update; the authors report a 21.5% improvement on long-form benchmarks. The fetch tool flagged that the claim to beat all baselines is not fully supported by the paper's own Table 1, where the strongest results come from a variant that updates a layer by gradient descent and is not closed-form. No independent replication of the 21.5% was found, and the authors say the method is "not explicitly optimized for lifelong editing" ([AnyEdit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05628); [full text mirror](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.05628.md)).

Almost every other closed-form successor is one extra term or constraint on the same least-squares problem: a ridge penalty on the update (ENCORE), a cap on the edited matrix's condition number (PRUNE, ICLR 2025), regularization by relative weight change (RECT, EMNLP 2024), orthogonalised or spectrally filtered updates (O-Edit; REVIVE, ACL 2026, abstract only) ([ENCORE](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01636v1); [PRUNE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16821); [RECT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04700); [O-Edit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.11469); [REVIVE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11042)). Four more (DeltaEdit, AdaEdit, IWCKEdit, SeqMMR) were seen only as search listings and are not described. The alternatives leave the main weights alone (GRACE's codebook, WISE's side memory) or return to fine-tuning: LocFT-BF (ICLR 2026) claims ordinary mini-batch fine-tuning on localized parameters beats locate-then-edit methods and sustains 100,000 edits, by its authors' account ([GRACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11031); [WISE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14768); [LocFT-BF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22072)). The field has not converged.

### Six documented failure modes

**Sequential edits forget, then collapse.** Gupta, Rao and Anumanchipalli (Findings of ACL 2024; abstract only) describe "an initial gradual but progressive forgetting phase followed by abrupt or catastrophic forgetting"; Yang et al. (Findings of ACL 2024; abstract only) report that "even a single edit can trigger model collapse"; RECT and PRUNE tie the damage to excessive weight change and a growing condition number; AlphaEdit's own baselines show MEMIT at 34.62 efficacy after 2,000 sequential edits ([arXiv 2401.07453](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07453); [arXiv 2402.09656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09656); [AlphaEdit](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2410.02355.md)). Gupta et al. (2025) add that edited-matrix norms grow continuously, more than tenfold for MEMIT over 10,000 edits on Llama3-8B, and call the result "importance hacking", a causal story so far from one group; the same paper and a separate ICLR 2025 paper report "editing overfit", edited facts pushed to probabilities near 1 where unedited models recall known facts at roughly 39% to 52% ([arXiv 2502.01636](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01636v1); [arXiv 2410.07819](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07819)).

**ROME's reference code used two different keys in one formula.** The intended update uses the prefix-averaged key throughout; the released implementation used the un-prefixed key in the residual and the denominator, which lets the denominator approach zero and the update explode into a "disabling edit". The corrected r-ROME (EMNLP 2024) survives 5,000 sequential edits on GPT-J at a score of 80.20 where the original collapses at 57.53, though gradual degradation remains "an inherent limitation of ROME that we do not overcome". A second group reached the same diagnosis independently (abstract only), and the EasyEdit library's maintainers acknowledged ROME and MEMIT bugs ([r-ROME](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07175v3); [arXiv 2406.11263](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11263); [survey note](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01286)).

**Edits do not propagate to consequences.** RippleEdits (TACL 2024) finds editors "fail to introduce consistent changes in the model's knowledge", with an in-context baseline scoring best; MQuAKE (EMNLP 2023) finds they "fail catastrophically" on multi-hop questions; a 2025 work-in-progress preprint (abstract only) reports collapse "even under the simplest negation queries" ([RippleEdits](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12976); [MQuAKE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14795); [arXiv 2510.00625](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00625)).

**Causal tracing does not pick the best layer.** Hase et al. (NeurIPS 2023; abstract only) find localization results "do not provide any insight into which model MLP layer would be best to edit" ([arXiv 2301.04213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04213)).

**Standard evaluation leaked the answer.** "The Mirage of Model Editing" (Yang et al.; ACL 2025) shows that teacher forcing and truncating output to the ground-truth length inflate results; under free generation judged for correctness, single-edit success is **38.5% against 96.8%**, and over 1,000 sequential edits ROME falls from 0.114 to 0.001 and MEMIT from 0.057 to 0.002 reliability. AlphaEdit was not evaluated ([Mirage](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2502.11177.md); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.745)). A 2025 preprint by He et al. reaches the same verdict with a contextual baseline that "consistently outperforms" parameter editors ([arXiv 2505.18690](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18690)).

**Later fine-tuning erodes edits.** Cheng et al. (KDD 2026; abstract only) find across 254 configurations that "edits decay substantially after subsequent fine-tuning" ([arXiv 2511.05852](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05852)).

No 2025-2026 source opened concludes that parameter editing is reliable at scale; WikiBigEdit (ICML 2025) reports, per a search summary of its text, that retrieval and continual fine-tuning outperform editors on real edit streams ([WikiBigEdit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05683)). Method papers that claim 10,000 or more edits (ENCORE, BetaEdit, REVIVE) are judged by the older probability-based yardstick; until someone else evaluates them under the stricter protocol the two sets of numbers should not be compared. No dedicated 2025 or 2026 survey of model editing was found, and the three 2023-2024 surveys were verified at abstract level only.

## An 8x8 layer reproduces every edit's arithmetic and none of the search

Everything in this section is analysis from the research notes, not literature: toy designs constructed from the cited mechanisms, plus one set of numerical checks whose specific figures come from a single random seed and are illustrative. No source provides a toy-scale reference implementation of any of these methods.

The central finding of that analysis is a split. The weight change in every system above is small, exact array arithmetic: a rank-one projection, a zeroed column, a rank-one outer product, a least-squares solve, a projector. All of it fits one 8x8 matrix. What does not fit is what the papers are actually about: finding where and what to edit inside a deep nonlinear model, obtaining `k*` and `v*` from a real network, and, for the one direct result, the open-ended reasoning of an LLM. A second split matters for honesty: algebraic failure modes (interference between edits, ill-conditioning, mismatched keys, an absent null space) live in the single matrix and can be reproduced; semantic failure modes (ripple effects, multi-hop, negation, evaluation leakage) concern what a key and value mean inside a language model and can only be stated with citations.

| Direct and closed-form edits | Toy at 8x8 | "AI" as a fixed policy | What the toy omits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction removal (the documented 10-pass formula) | Plant a unit direction `u` that `W` writes to on some inputs; `W' = W - a u (u^T W)`; at `a = 1`, `u^T W' = 0` exactly | Take a few bad and good inputs, `u = normalize(mean(y_bad) - mean(y_good))`, edit a copy, re-measure a defect score and a keep-intact score, keep only if the first improves and the second holds | Locating layers and columns among 24 layers; nonlinearity and superposition; looping as a property of long sampled text; the 20-hour search |
| Column-restricted removal (the 50-pass edit, described in words) | Apply the subtraction to the `k` columns with largest `abs(u^T w_j)`; edit 2 of 8 | Rank columns, sweep `k` | Why few columns suffice in a real model is an empirical fact about Qwen; a toy shows it only if planted |
| Component switch-off | Zero a block of columns | Ablate each block, pick the one that most reduces the defect | Attention heads; in the source this was the weaker edit |
| Reading weights | Column norms of a first layer shown as a grid | Norms, then nearest labeled template | The inference from a heat map to a behavior; the failed sycophancy case |
| Writing weights from scratch | Hand-set a matrix to a permutation, shift or comparison | A fixed compiler from a tiny spec | Designing the program, which is the claimed capability |
| Heretic-style search | Choose `a` and `k` by search on defect score plus a drift penalty | Grid, then random or evolutionary search | The TPE optimizer; refusal measured on text |
| Select-and-verify loop (Barez's proposal) | Offer several candidate edits; score fix rate, related and unrelated side effects; choose; probe for re-emergence | Evaluate all, pick by fixed rule, always keep the original | The human expert, localization tools; and nothing in the agenda has been built |
| ROME rank-one edit | Exact: edited key hits its value to rounding error, update has rank one | Not applicable (a formula) | Where `k*`, `v*` and the layer come from; `C` estimated from text |
| MEMIT batch | Exact least squares; `λ` trades fit against preservation | Not applicable | Multi-layer spreading; keys recomputed per layer |
| AlphaEdit projection | Exact when preserved keys span fewer than 8 dimensions | Not applicable | The approximate null space of real layers, shown only by thresholding small eigenvalues |
| Sequential degradation | Each edit exact when made; earlier edits overwritten | A gate can accept, shrink or reject | The rate: interference at 8 dimensions is far stronger than in thousands |

The numerical checks behind the closed-form rows, to be regenerated and not quoted, ran as follows. With 40 stored keys, the ROME update met its constraint to about 1e-15 and disturbed the stored keys 3.8 times less than the plain `C = I` update, while changing the weights more (the `C`-weighted update minimizes change on stored keys, not weight change). MEMIT's closed form equalled a generic least-squares solver to machine precision, and for a single key equals ROME's update shrunk by `s/(1+s)` with `s = k^T C_0^-1 k` (the note-taker's reconstruction, confirmed numerically). With five preserved keys in eight dimensions, the projected update left them untouched to about 1e-14 where MEMIT moved them, but the paper's `+ I` regularizer left the new keys only half fitted at this scale, so a demo should expose that strength. With 40 preserved keys no null space exists and the projector is zero; thresholding the smallest directions allowed a partial edit with 3.7% to 5.5% leakage, the approximate-null-space critique in miniature. Over 24 sequential ROME edits each was exact when made, yet the first edit's relative error was 4.9 right after the second, and drift on the original keys rose from 7% to 46%.

Three conditions must be stated on any such page: `C` must be invertible or carry a ridge term; with eight or more independent stored keys exact preservation of all of them is impossible; and the norm growth reported for real models did not appear monotonically in the toy run, so a page should not claim to reproduce "the norm always increases" unless it is built to and says so. Sequential AlphaEdit, the condition-number argument and the mismatched-key blow-up were not run numerically.

Two cautions from that analysis carry real weight. Whether a small linear MEND editor measurably beats one tuned gradient step on an 8x8 layer is untested; the closed-form reasoning says a locality gain is available when inputs are correlated and none when they are isotropic, so the result must be measured before it is promised. And a Text-to-LoRA toy will flatter the method unless its tasks are built so that a single shared adapter is also competitive, with the averaged adapter and the shared adapter included as controls and both training schemes run so the sign-and-scale ambiguity of independently trained adapters is visible.

## Recommendation: direction removal first, a gated fact edit second, a recipe policy third

### First: "contrast, project, check", with a matrix that has nothing to fix

This is the only demo that reproduces a published instance of an AI editing weights directly, and it does so with the formula as documented. **What it shows.** An 8x8 layer carries a planted defect: on some inputs it writes along a direction `u`. A policy takes a few bad and good examples, computes `u` from the difference of mean outputs using forward passes only, applies `W' = W - a u (u^T W)` to a copy, and re-measures a defect score and a keep-intact score on inputs it did not use to find `u`. It keeps the edit only if the first improved and the second did not degrade, and otherwise restores the original. Sweeping `a` shows exact removal at 1 and over-correction at 1.5; restricting the edit to the two columns that write `u` most shows "a tiny fraction of the weights" in miniature. Then the same policy runs on a second matrix with no planted defect and achieves nothing, which is the source's Gemma result and the half of the story most coverage dropped. The rails are the ones the sources actually used: a disposable copy, a list of refused edit types, a counted evaluation budget, a held-out check, keep-only-if-better.

**What it must say it does not show.** It does not show how to find the layer, the columns or the direction in a deep nonlinear model, where a direction is not cleanly one behavior and looping is a property of long sampled text. A fixed policy is faithful to Heretic and to contrast-based editing, whose deciders are fixed algorithms; it is not faithful to Weight-Space Programming, whose claim is that an LLM's reasoning replaced the fixed policy. The page should present the formula as the documented 10-pass edit and the column-restricted form as the 50-pass edit described in words, because the headline edit's strength and direction-finding method are unverified. And it must date its source: an unreviewed, unreplicated blog post whose gain was a defect removed, not an ability added.

**What it needs.** Matrix products, norms and a sort; no inverse and no training. The "AI" is a fixed policy first; a grid search over `a` and `k` against defect score plus a drift penalty is a natural second stage and stands in for Heretic's optimizer.

### Second: edit one association in closed form, then let a gate decide over a stream of edits

This is the mechanism the field would hand such an agent, shown together with the reason nobody trusts it unsupervised. **What it shows.** A layer as a key-to-value memory; the ROME rank-one edit hitting its target exactly; the same constraint met without `C` disturbing other keys more; MEMIT's batch form and its `λ` trade-off; projection leaving preserved keys untouched when a null space exists and leaking when it does not. Then a stream of edits: each exact when made, earlier ones overwritten, drift accumulating. Finally a policy in the shape of Barez's proposal and STABLE's gate: several candidate edits applied to copies, scored on the target, on related keys and on unrelated keys, the best accepted, shrunk or rejected against a budget, the original always kept. Running the stream with and without the gate shows what verification buys and what it costs in evaluations.

**What it must say it does not show.** The toy is handed `k*` and `v*`, so "W k* = v*" carries no meaning about facts; the real methods find the layer by causal tracing (which does not predict the best layer), the key from activations, the value by gradient descent through the network, and `C` from sampled text. The semantic failures (consequences not propagating, negation, leaked evaluation, erosion by fine-tuning) cannot be simulated and must be stated with their citations, above all the 38.5% against 96.8% result. Interference at eight dimensions is far faster than in a real layer, so the toy shows the mechanism and not the rate. And no published system has an LLM choose among these editors: the loop is a proposal.

**What it needs.** A matrix inverse or linear solve; at least eight independent stored keys or a ridge term so `C` is invertible; fewer preserved keys than dimensions for exact projection, with the regularizer strength exposed. The published projector is built from an eigen-decomposition. As an observation of this report, to be confirmed by the demo itself, the exact case needs only a solve, since the projector off the preserved keys is `I - K_0 (K_0^T K_0)^-1 K_0^T` when those keys are independent; the approximate, thresholded case does need a spectrum. The "AI" is a fixed policy.

### Third: a policy that learns which recipe to apply, then forgets and collapses

This is the SEAL loop in tabular form, and the only candidate in which the "AI" learns. **What it shows.** Two loops: sample a recipe from a menu, apply it to a copy by a few gradient steps, score the copy on a held-out pair, discard it; then move the policy toward the recipes that were rewarded. The page can report SEAL's own three-way comparison (untrained policy, trained policy, hand-picked oracle), show harmful recipes being filtered out, and then show the two regressions the follow-up papers document: earlier tasks degrading once updates are kept, with a gate as remedy, and the policy collapsing onto one recipe.

**What it must say it does not show.** The inner loop is gradient descent, so this is self-directed fine-tuning and not direct editing; it belongs under the heading only as the best-known neighbor. The generator is a separate table and not the model being edited, the variant SEAL mentions and never ran, so the "self" is missing unless a shared-weights version is built. There is no language, the menu is hand-made so the policy cannot invent a strategy, and the result says nothing about whether the loop works for language models, where a good human-written prompt still matches the learned policy.

**What it needs.** Gradient steps on squared error, a seeded sampler and a table of probabilities. The "AI" is a learned tabular policy; a fixed policy would remove the point of the demo.

Held in reserve are a MEND toy, the cleanest picture of "a second network writes a rank-one change" but one whose benefit at this scale is unmeasured; a description-to-adapter toy in Zhyper's form, which fits better beside lessons on adapters and needs its strong controls; and the singular-value and context-identity toys, which are exact but are selection and equivalence respectively. Drag-and-Drop, diffusion over weights (except as a demonstration of memorization), SDFT, Absolute Zero and Irregular's scenario are not recommended as computed demos; Irregular's findings are worth stating in prose wherever rails are discussed.

## Conclusion: seven questions the repository owner must settle

**What may be called "the AI" on the page?** A fixed policy is honest for Heretic-style editing and dishonest for Weight-Space Programming unless the page says so. At 8x8 a real LLM can read all 64 numbers, which is closer to the source's small-network experiments than any toy policy; the owner must decide whether a live or recorded LLM run ever belongs in a repository where every number is otherwise computed in the array language, and how such a run would be kept reproducible.

**Is a three-day-old blog post a sound foundation for a lesson?** The first demo's arithmetic is safe regardless, since the same projection underlies abliteration and contrast-based editing. Its framing depends on a source with no peer review, no replication and no arXiv version. The owner can build now with a dated caveat, or wait for a paper or a replication.

**Who verifies the headline edit before a page prints it?** The 50-pass edit's strength and direction-finding method sit in logs and a manifest that were not opened. Opening them is a small, bounded task that would turn "described in words" into "documented".

**Does the direction-removal demo come before or after closed-form editing?** It needs no inverse and no notion of keys and values, so it can stand first; placed after, it can reuse the vocabulary of locality and retention. The answer changes what each page may assume.

**How much linear algebra will the array language carry?** Exact projection needs only a solve, but the approximate null space that real layers have, and the leakage critique, need eigenvalues. The owner must choose between showing only the exact case and saying so, or waiting for a spectrum.

**What will the repository call the topic?** No established term exists. A term of its own ("agent-directed weight editing" collides with nothing found) keeps "Weight-Space Programming" for its authors' sense and avoids implying a field that is not yet there.

**Which figures get re-checked at source before they appear in teaching pages?** Table cells for the hypernetwork papers came through a summarizer; several papers are known at abstract level only; the AlphaEdit, ENCORE and Transformer² formulas are one step removed from verbatim; and every toy number came from one seed. The literature's clearest lesson is that the stricter the check, the smaller the claim, and a teaching repository that plants its own defects and hands itself its own keys is in the same position: its pages are trustworthy to the degree that each says what was planted, what was assumed and what was only read about.
