  • program demos/dynamic-mixing/dynamic-mixing.xtl: Dynamic mixing: the paper's own mixing tables (Xie and others, arXiv:2512.24880, eq. 7 to 9), made afresh for every input. The stream state X (S = 4 streams of C = 8) is flattened to one list of 32 and scaled to a root-mean-square of 1 (RMS normalization); one projection phi (32 x 24, the paper's fused form, its eq. 14) gives 4 + 4 + 16 numbers; each group is multiplied by a gating factor alpha (the paper starts them at 0.01) and a bias is added; then H_pre = sigmoid, H_post = 2 sigmoid, H_res = Sinkhorn-Knopp of the exponential, 20 rounds. The layer is the paper's eq. 3: the block's input is H_pre X (a weighted sum of the streams), and the new state is H_res X + H_post' f. Part 1: two inputs, their tables, at the gating factor set; the stages of the first input are part 1's printout, which the page steps through. Part 3: the gradient of a probe of the new state by alpha, phi, the biases and the input, written out (through the Sinkhorn rounds, the sigmoids, the projection and the normalization) and checked against central differences. Written out here: ask FM13 of X_eTaL-ML (hc:d_ynamic).

  • program demos/lora-hello/lora-hello.xtl: LoRA hello: what does a small adapter add to a layer whose weights do not change? The question behind it: how can a model learn something new without every one of its numbers being retrained (the cost) or overwritten (the forgetting)? LoRA's answer is to freeze the weights and train a small correction beside them. Here is one layer of that, shrunk to 4 rows and 4 columns so every number can be read: a frozen table W and an adapter of rank 1, with X_eTaL-ML's LoRA library. Nothing is trained here (the adapter's numbers are made up): this shows what an adapter is, where it sits, what it adds and what it costs. The layer is y = W x + s B (A x): W stays as it is, and only A (1 row of 4) and B (1 column of 4) would be trained.
  • program demos/lora-lab/lora-lab.xtl: LoRA lab: how an adapter's correction is worked out, number by number, and what its rank changes. The layer is y = W x + s B (A x), written here as a pipeline of four stages so that every stage's numbers can be shown: the input x; the squeeze h = A x (r numbers); the spread u = B h (8 numbers); and the output y = W x + s u. The layer is the one of X_eTaL-ML's LoRA explorer (its W, A, B and x, made up by fixed ripples). Nothing is trained here.

  • program demos/lora-training/lora-training.xtl: LoRA training: where the numbers of A and B come from. A frozen 8 x 8 layer W; examples (inputs with the outputs wanted) made by a hidden shift of it, W + T; an adapter that starts as LoRA does, B at 0 and A seeded. One step of training as a pipeline, stage by stage: the prediction, the error (which is the loss's gradient at the output), the gradients of A and B, the update; checked against the library's own step, W untouched.   • program demos/lora-vs-fa/lora-vs-fa.xtl: LoRA beside LoRA-FA, in two experiments kept apart. Experiment A, approximating a known matrix: both fit a known rank-4 shift T from the same seeded A, B at 0; LoRA moves A and B, LoRA-FA moves B alone. The best any update of the rank can do (the Eckart-Young optimum, from T's singular values), and the floor LoRA-FA cannot pass: its update s B A has its rows in the row space of the frozen A, a subspace, so the closest it can come to T is T's projection onto that subspace. Each learned update is projected onto the row space and the remainder measured. Experiment B, learning from examples: the task of lesson 3 (examples made by the hidden shift, some held out), LoRA, LoRA-FA and full fine-tuning of W itself, with the loss on the examples trained on, on the held-out ones, and on old examples, what the layer gave before any training, measured after it (retention).

  • program demos/residual-streams/residual-streams.xtl: Residual streams: why a deep network needs a skip connection, and what happens when there are four of them. Part 1: a list of 8 numbers sent through 48 small layers (a block 8 -> 16 -> 8 with GELU, its weights fixed by a formula, a different table for every layer), once with each layer replacing the list by the block's output and once with each layer adding the block's output to the list it was given (the residual, or skip, connection); the size of the list after every layer, both ways. Part 2: Hyper-Connections (Xie and others, arXiv:2512.24880): the list widened to S = 4 streams, a 4 x 8 table, and one layer of it as a pipeline, stage by stage: H_pre mixes the streams, the merge averages them, the block runs once on the average, H_post spreads its output back over the streams, H_res mixes the carried state, and the two are added. The mixing tables start from the logits 2 I - 1 (the Python's start); H_res is their exponential (HC) or its Sinkhorn-Knopp projection (mHC: rows and columns each summing to 1). The expressions are written out here (ask FM13 of X_eTaL-ML, docs/xetal-ML-asks.md).
  • program demos/sinkhorn/sinkhorn.xtl: Sinkhorn-Knopp: a table whose rows and columns each sum to 1. mHC (Xie and others, arXiv:2512.24880) makes its carried-state mixing table from a positive 4 x 4 by dividing every row by its sum, then every column by its sum, twenty times over (its eq. 9, t_max = 20). Part 1: a positive 4 x 4 (the exponential of a table of logits: the paper's and the Python's start 2 I - 1, or a start set up as data), its row and column sums, and one round of the two divisions as a pipeline with every number. Part 2: the twenty rounds, the tables and their sums after every half round, how far the sums are from 1 after each round, and the count of rounds to within a millionth. Part 3: the three properties the paper states of the result (section 4.1), checked by the Check library: its largest singular value is at most 1 (a list multiplied by it does not grow; power iteration written out, ask FL2 of X_eTaL-libraries), the product of two such tables is one too, and it is a weighted mix of permutation tables (Birkhoff), found by a greedy search over the 24 orderings. The Sinkhorn loop is written out here (ask FM13 of X_eTaL-ML, docs/xetal-ML-asks.md).

  • program demos/training-at-depth/training-at-depth.xtl: Training at depth: the plain skip, Hyper-Connections (HC) and mHC (Xie and others, arXiv:2512.24880) trained on one tiny task, with Adam, from the same start. The task is the Python re-implementation's (mHC-poc), made tiny: each of 16 tokens, its successor the target (token + 1, 16 wrapping round to 1). A token is looked up in an embedding (16 x 8), sent through L layers, the streams averaged and scaled to a size of 1 (RMS), and turned into 16 scores by an unembedding (8 x 16): softmax and cross-entropy. Each layer is lesson D1's: H_pre mixes the S streams, their average goes through a block 8 -> 16 -> 8 with GELU, H_post hands its output back, H_res mixes the carried state. Every weight is trained: the blocks, the embeddings, and each layer's three tables of logits. The scheme is two settings: S = 1 and the projection on is the plain skip (every table is the number 1); S = 4 with H_res the exponential of its logits is HC; with its Sinkhorn projection, mHC. The gradient is written out, layer by layer, the Sinkhorn rounds included, and checked against central differences in the same program; FM13 of X_eTaL-ML (hc:l_ayer, hc:b_ackTop, hc:b_ackBottom, hc:s_inkhornBack) is the ask (docs/xetal-ML-asks.md).

X_eTaL-ML's Flow runs the pipeline and can report every stage's value, which is what a page steps through.
